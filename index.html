About

Where commerce, ethics and the environment meet.

I am completing a Bachelor of Commerce in International Business and Sustainability Ethics at Victoria University of Wellington, graduating November 2026. My work sits at the intersection of global commerce, ethical sustainability and biodiversity, and I am most interested in what happens when those three are in tension rather than alignment.

Academically that has meant policy and regulatory analysis, ESG disclosure, crisis and resilience theory, systems thinking and quantitative methods. In practice it has meant co-authoring research on Pacific climate adaptation finance, researching how the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism lands on New Zealand exporters, comparing how two energy companies actually report against GRI standards, and mapping the stakeholder network behind Wellington's electricity distribution.

Alongside study I contribute to WEAll Aotearoa as a Research and Engagement Volunteer, supporting research, policy review and the development of resources that advance a wellbeing economy.

Long-term, I aspire to contribute to sustainability and climate policy while building initiatives that advance animal welfare, biodiversity protection, and ethical development.

Featured case study

Fire and Coral.

Volcanic soil fertility, atoll vulnerability, and the case for geologically differentiated climate adaptation in the Pacific. Co-authored with Rohit Gunasekaran, Victoria University of Wellington. Selected for poster presentation at the 5th Pacific Climate Change Conference, Honiara.

Problem

Pacific adaptation planning treats island states as one vulnerability class. Their soils are not one class, and uniform instruments therefore misfire in both directions.

Insight

Parent material determines recovery capacity. The same cyclone or eruption is a recoverable shock on volcanic soil and a permanent loss on carbonate sand.

Recommendation

Screen on parent material. Soil geology belongs alongside exposure and income in adaptation appraisal, not in the background description.

Allocation already diverges

Climate ODA per person, 2010 to 2014 commitments. Source: SEI (2017).
Tuvalu atollUS$3,835
Kiribati atollUS$276
Papua New Guinea mixedUS$6

Two atoll states with near identical land and water constraints differ by a factor of 14. The spread has no stated geological rationale. Naming one would make it defensible.

Recovery capacity after a shock

Depth of profile, nutrient resupply and water storage determine whether fertility returns.
High volcanic islandFiji · Samoa · Tonga
Recoverable
Low coral atollTuvalu · Kiribati · Marshall Is.
Threshold loss

The asymmetry. Rain flushes tsunami salt down the deep volcanic profile, and the ash left behind is cation rich. The same salt load on carbonate sand has nowhere to drain and leaves no fertility dividend.

Hunga Tonga, 2022: fertility delivered by disaster

Volcanism cuts both ways. Sources: World Bank GRADE (2022); FAO (2022); CSIRO (2022).
18.5%of GDP lost
85%of farming households affected
20–30 mmashfall on Tongatapu
5–10 yrto flush salts, then fertility returns

Agricultural damage reached US$20.9 million, about 80 percent of it from ash rather than the tsunami. Root crops survived; leaf and fruit crops did not. Depth is the buffer.

The advantage is being spent

Potassium balance at national scale, kg K per hectare per year. Every bar left of zero is net depletion. Source: ACIAR SMCN/2016/111.

The volcanic advantage was inherited rather than earned, and it is being spent faster than it is replaced: 30 to 50 percent of soil carbon lost in two decades, and a taro yield gap of 2,050 kg dry matter per hectare in Samoa.

Two soils, two food systems

The divide appears only when soil and water are measured directly.

Headline statistics hide the divide. Agriculture is 23.2 percent of GDP in Kiribati but 9.1 percent in Samoa, because fisheries and copra dominate atoll accounts.
